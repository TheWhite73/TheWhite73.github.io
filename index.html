About

Looking for the simplest explanation

I came to mathematics out of curiosity. I wanted the simplest explanation of a particular phenomenon, and mathematics turned out to be where that search leads. At first algebra had me completely. I was reading category theory and homological algebra, I expected a career on the pure side, and Atiyah and Grothendieck were the people I looked up to.

Working at the UNAM changed the direction. Topological data analysis was new to me, and what caught my attention was seeing mathematics that sophisticated used to explain something concrete: the geometric structure hiding inside a set of data.

Then I won a scholarship for an exchange semester at Saint Mary's University, and took a turn: graphs, network analysis, numerical analysis, artificial intelligence. Not long after, I was one of 72 people awarded a Samsung Innovation Campus scholarship to study machine learning and the uses it is put to.

Like any mathematician, though, I was left wanting to know what sits inside the black box. That became my undergraduate thesis: universal approximation theorems, from Cybenko and sigmoidal functions through to Anderson's results for multivariate functions. It is what gave me an appetite for measure theory, functional analysis and PDEs.

A Globalink scholarship took me from there to a research internship at Simon Fraser University in Vancouver, working on inverse problems. My part was building models in PyTorch to find algorithms that come close to the theoretical bounds on our covering numbers for a given distribution.

What I like

Probability and measure theory underneath, inverse problems and diffusion models on top, and the question that keeps pulling me back: what did the model actually learn?

Probability & measure the ground floor

A generative model is a map that pushes a simple measure onto a complicated one. Measure theory is where I go when I need to say precisely what that sentence means.

Inverse problems ill-posedness

A forward operator between Hilbert spaces, and the question that follows it: when the map smooths, recovering x from y is ill-posed and regularisation stops being optional.

Diffusion models generative

Noise the data along an SDE, learn the score, run it backwards. What interests me is the theory underneath it: which distributions this can reach, and how many measurements it takes to get there.

Interpretability the black box

Universal approximation says a network can represent the function. It says nothing about what this network learned. That gap is my thesis, and the reason I did not stop at using the tools.

Jun 2026 – Sep 2026Simon Fraser University, Vancouver

Covering numbers for Bayesian inverse problems

Mitacs Globalink Research Internship · supervised by Prof. Ben Adcock

  • Built models in PyTorch searching for algorithms whose numerics come close to the theoretical bounds on the covering number of a given distribution, tested mainly on Fashion-MNIST and CelebA.
  • Modelled and generalised Bayesian priors so that the covering number of the underlying distribution becomes a usable measure of how complex a dataset is.
  • Scoped an open question with no predefined solution path: distilled a dense prior literature into a working framework and isolated which assumptions actually drove the results.
  • Still ongoing. We are extending the results to sparsity-promoting priors, with Ben Adcock, Nick Huang, Maksym Neyra-Nesterenko and Omar Pacheco.

An AI diagnostic system that classifies chest radiographs as bacterial pneumonia, viral pneumonia, or normal. Severe class imbalance was handled by oversampling the underrepresented normal and viral categories with a GAN, then fine-tuning a VGG16 backbone for multi-class classification.
